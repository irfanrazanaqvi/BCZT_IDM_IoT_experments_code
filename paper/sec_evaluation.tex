\section{Experimental Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Methodology and What the Numbers Mean}\label{sec:metrics}
We report latency, throughput, on-chain gas, revocation exposure (requests accepted after revocation), per-tier acceptance of legitimate and attacking requests, quorum behaviour, recall capacity and degraded-mode behaviour. Three limits apply throughout. (i) Testbed timings are \emph{compute-bound}: they exclude network, block time and multi-node consensus. (ii) Sections~\ref{sec:scaling}--\ref{sec:outage_eval} use \emph{synthetic} devices, signals and attacks, so they characterize policy mechanics under stated overlaps; Section~\ref{sec:real_eval} replaces the behavioural signal with detector scores from real TON\_IoT traffic, while posture and network context stay synthetic. (iii) One two-vCPU host was used.

\subsection{Testbed Performance and Repeated-Run Statistics}\label{sec:scaling}
Table~\ref{tab:results} gives one run per fleet size. Mean authorization latency is 0.120--0.197\,ms and registration 0.173--0.286\,ms; the two smallest fleets are slower (0.184 and 0.197\,ms against 0.120--0.127\,ms for $n\ge200$), probably from warm-up. Beyond $n=200$ latency stays flat as the fleet grows eightfold, which fits a constant per-request cost dominated by one Ed25519 verification, and throughput is 3{,}700--6{,}200 decisions/s. Thirty repeats at $n=400$ give a mean authorization latency of $0.133\pm0.013$\,ms (95\% CI 0.128--0.138) and registration $0.184\pm0.019$\,ms. Over 20{,}000 sequential decisions the percentiles are p50 0.124, p95 0.188, p99 0.247 and p99.9 0.932\,ms, with a 4.3\,ms maximum from scheduling or garbage collection. Intervals assume roughly normal run means and apply to this host.

\subsection{On-Chain Cost: Measured EVM Gas}\label{sec:gas}
Table~\ref{tab:gas} lists gas measured over 50 devices on the Hardhat EVM; gas is deterministic given the state path (s.d.\ below 10). Registration costs about 215k gas and a full audited decision about 70k, mostly one \texttt{ecrecover} and one new nonce slot. Requests denied for replay (41.5k) or against a revoked device (39.3k) are cheaper because they exit early; denials after the oracle check cost as much as an allow. The industrial additions cost almost nothing: an ALLOW on a strict resource (70.4k), an anomaly-gate denial (70.2k), a strict-borderline denial (70.4k) and a below-threshold denial (70.2k) are all within 0.3\% of the base decision. Anchoring a decision-log root costs 70.8k gas the first time and 53.7k in steady state, or 54 gas per decision at $B=1000$ decisions per root, about three orders of magnitude below deciding each one on-chain (70.4k).

\subsection{Hybrid Enforcement: Cost of the Fast Path}\label{sec:fast_eval}
Over 10 runs of 2{,}000 mixed T0/T2 requests the contract model, which verifies one signature, takes $0.134\pm0.020$\,ms (95\% CI 0.119--0.148) and the fast path, which verifies the device signature and the oracle attestation, $0.261\pm0.022$\,ms (CI 0.245--0.277), about 3{,}800 decisions/s on one core. This is a cost, not a gain: the fast path needs no transaction, gas or block, and what that is worth depends on network and consensus latency that this test did not measure.

Four observations follow. First, naive compromise is denied at every tier (0\% accepted), so it does not test the design. Second, at T0 an attacker that spoofs signals is accepted 100\% of the time under any policy. This is by design, since T0 is read-only telemetry, but a compromised device that keeps looking normal can read T0 data. Third, the gate and strict mode matter where consequences are high: at T2 the full-spoof attacker falls from 100\% to 0\% accepted and the stealthy attacker from 98.5\% to 60.9\%; at T3 from 52.4\% to 0\% and from 52.2\% to 13.6\%. Fourth, there is a price. Legitimate allowance drops from 99.9\% to 97.8\% at T2 and from 96.0\% to 94.8\% at T3 (5.2\% of legitimate T3 requests are denied outright), because upset requests exceed the gate and strict mode refuses borderline scores. A plant must decide whether that is acceptable for setpoint and firmware writes, where a second confirmation is usually welcome.

In an ablation of the trust signals, the full score separates partial and stealthy mimicry with AUC 0.993 and 0.895, whereas dropping the behavioural signal leaves both at AUC 0.499--0.500: the behavioural signal alone separates mimicry from legitimate traffic in this setup. Posture and network are drawn from the same ranges for both populations in the synthetic fleet, so removing them \emph{raises} AUC. That argues for adaptive rather than fixed weighting; it is not evidence that real posture is uninformative.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the Anomaly Gate}\label{sec:gate_eval}
Fig.~\ref{fig:iiotres}(b) sweeps the gate level from 0.15 to 0.60 for legitimate upset rates of 5\% and 15\% (0\% gives no false denials by construction). At 5\%, a gate of 0.30 blocks 40.1\% of stealthy mimicry and 100\% of full spoofing while denying 2.3\% of legitimate requests. Tightening to 0.25 and 0.20 raises the stealthy block rate to 60.4\% and 80.4\% at 3.1\% and 3.8\% legitimate denials, and 0.15 blocks all of it at 4.6\%. Loosening to 0.40 blocks no stealthy attacker and lets 25\% of full spoofing through, rising to 74\% at 0.60. At a 15\% upset rate, gates of 0.30 and 0.20 deny 7.4\% and 12.2\% of legitimate requests. The gate is a genuine trade-off whose operating point depends on how noisy legitimate behaviour is; because the attack range $[0.15,0.40]$ overlaps legitimate upsets by construction, an attacker that keeps its anomaly below the gate is accepted, as Proposition~\ref{prop:bound}(d) predicts.

\subsection{Evaluation on Real Traffic: TON\_IoT}\label{sec:real_eval}
The synthetic study fixes the anomaly distributions by hand, so we repeat the central experiments with $A$ produced by detectors on a public dataset. TON\_IoT provides labelled IIoT telemetry \cite{alsaedi2020ton} and network flows \cite{moustafa2021network}, whose heterogeneity is analysed in \cite{booij2022ton}; free academic use is allowed if the dataset papers are cited. We used four telemetry files (Fridge, Modbus, Thermostat, Weather) and the network file (211{,}043 flows, 190{,}474 after removing 20{,}569 exact duplicates; nine attack types plus normal traffic).

\emph{Method.} Features exclude IP addresses, source ports and, for telemetry, dates and times (normal and attack records were collected on different days). A gradient-boosted classifier (supervised) and an Isolation Forest trained on normal records only (unsupervised) are evaluated on a stratified 70/30 split over five seeds; to approximate unseen attacks we also train the classifier with one attack type left out and score that type (seed 11). The attack probability is $A\in[0,1]$. Legitimate requests carry the score of a normal test flow; attackers carry the score of an attack flow and spoof posture and network context to 1 (the full-spoof attacker of Section~\ref{sec:devsim}). Posture, network context and the reliability warm-up stay synthetic, and because the files share no device identifier, assigning a flow-derived score to a device is arbitrary. The four tiers of Table~\ref{tab:tiers} are used unchanged.

\emph{Telemetry alone is a weak signal.} Table~\ref{tab:telemetry} shows that only Weather separates attacks usefully (supervised AUC 0.972, Isolation Forest 0.819); Modbus gives 0.623 and 0.498, and Thermostat and Fridge are near chance (0.513 and 0.531 supervised). Before whitespace stripping, the Fridge column \texttt{temp\_condition} carries label-specific padding that gives a perfect AUC of 1.0, an encoding artefact, so we strip all strings before training. A plant's behavioural signal should therefore come from traffic, not only from sensor values.

\emph{Network flows are a strong signal, with a caveat.} On flows the supervised classifier reaches AUC 0.9999 (s.d.\ below $10^{-4}$) and the Isolation Forest $0.916\pm0.004$, ranging from 0.813 (DDoS) to 0.956 (password attacks). Read the near-perfect supervised value as an upper bound: random splits of a benchmark whose attacks come from a few source hosts are optimistic, which is why we also test held-out types. With a type withheld, AUC against normal traffic stays at 0.990--1.000, but the share of that type's flows scored above 0.5 falls to 65.4\% for ransomware and 73.7\% for man-in-the-middle, against 93--100\% for most others. Legitimate flows mostly score near zero (median 0.0003), but the tail is heavy (99th percentile 0.27, 99.9th 0.96), so any gate cuts into some legitimate traffic.

\emph{Policy on real scores.} Table~\ref{tab:realpolicy} gives the percentage of attacker requests accepted, averaged over the nine attack types and, in the worst-case columns, for the single worst type, with threshold alone (Thr.) and with the full policy. Four findings follow. First, the full policy costs legitimate traffic little: allowance falls from 99.7, 99.5 and 99.0\% to 99.6, 98.8 and 98.4\% at T1, T2 and T3. Second, for attack types seen in training, the gate matters at T1 (mean acceptance 100\% $\to$ 0.7\%, worst 4.9\%, man-in-the-middle), whereas at T2 and T3 the high thresholds already reject nearly all such flows (1.1\% and 0.6\% with threshold alone), because a real detector scores them near 1 and Proposition~\ref{prop:bound}(d) then gives $T\approx0.7$ or below. This corrects the synthetic study, whose stealthy population sat at $A\le0.4$ and overstated how often the threshold alone fails on seen attacks. Third, for held-out types, residual acceptance grows to 11.9\% (T1), 7.9\% (T2) and 6.6\% (T3) on average, and to 41.6\%, 33.9\% and 35.8\% for the worst type (ransomware): a policy that depends on a detector inherits its blind spots. Fourth, an adaptive attacker that observes the detector and sends only its best-scoring flows is far more dangerous. Sending only the lowest-scoring 10\% of a held-out type's flows gives mean acceptance of 59.4, 37.9 and 29.0\% at T1--T3, and 76.5, 74.7 and 58.2\% for the best 1\%, with some types always accepted. This assumes the attacker can query the detector and craft traffic with a chosen score, which is a strong capability, so these numbers are a worst case.

\subsection{A Second Dataset: WUSTL-IIOT-2018 (SCADA)}\label{sec:wustl_eval}
To check that the gate behaviour is not specific to one collection, we repeat the policy experiment on WUSTL-IIOT-2018, SCADA flow records with injected attacks \cite{teixeira2018scada,teixeira2018wustl}. The file has 7{,}037{,}983 rows but only 62{,}959 distinct ones (99.1\% duplicates; 39{,}817 normal, 23{,}142 attack) and a binary label only, so we de-duplicate before splitting and cannot repeat the held-out-type test of Section~\ref{sec:real_eval}. We exclude the source port, keep five count features and use a stratified 70/30 split over five seeds.

A gradient-boosted classifier is almost perfect (AUC 0.99998, sd $2\times10^{-5}$) because attack flows here are short (median two packets) and normal flows are not, so the result says little about hard attacks. An Isolation Forest trained on normal flows reaches AUC $0.909\pm0.006$, a more realistic stand-in for an unseen attack; we map its score to $A\in[0,1]$ using normal training data alone (median to the 99.9th percentile).

Table~\ref{tab:wustl} gives the results. With the supervised score the full policy accepts at most 0.3\% of attack requests at T1--T3 and denies at most 0.2\% of legitimate ones, essentially the TON\_IoT picture. With the unsupervised score the gate and strict mode still cut attack acceptance at the two critical tiers from 100\% and 94.1\% (threshold only) to 8.2\% and 4.3\%, but legitimate allowance drops to 87.5\% at T2 and 82.2\% at T3, far above the 1--2\% cost with the supervised detector, because many normal flows look unusual to a detector fitted on five coarse counters. At T0 and T1 the unsupervised variant accepts all attack requests: the T1 gate is 0.60, above the median attack score of about 0.49. An adaptive attacker sending its best 10\% (1\%) of flows is accepted 86.9\% (100\%) of the time at T2 and 43.0\% (100\%) at T3 with the unsupervised score, against 2.1\% (16.0\%) and 0.7\% (6.3\%) with the supervised one. What carries over is the role of the gate; what does not is the availability of a strong detector. With a weak detector the policy trades a large share of legitimate availability for protection at the critical tiers. As with TON\_IoT, posture and network context are synthetic and the flow-to-device pairing is arbitrary.

\subsection{A Third Dataset With Attack Types: WUSTL-IIoT-2021}\label{sec:wustl21_eval}
WUSTL-IIoT-2021 contains Argus flow records from an industrial Modbus testbed with four labelled attack types \cite{zolanvari2019vulnerability,zolanvari2021wustl}, so it supports the held-out-type test that the SCADA file of Section~\ref{sec:wustl_eval} could not. It has 1{,}194{,}464 rows (1{,}107{,}448 normal; DoS 78{,}305, reconnaissance 8{,}240, command injection 259, backdoor 212), all captured on one day with attacks from two to four hosts. We drop timestamps, IP addresses, the source port, \texttt{IdleTime} (an epoch timestamp that separates types by capture time) and the IP-identification fields, keep 39 features, and remove 41{,}645 exact duplicates (1{,}152{,}819 rows remain). The evaluation follows Section~\ref{sec:real_eval}: stratified 70/30 splits over five seeds, a gradient-boosted classifier, an Isolation Forest on normal flows only with $A$ calibrated on normal training scores, and leave-one-type-out models as the held-out proxy.

\emph{Detection.} The supervised classifier is almost perfect on seen types (AUC 0.99999, unchanged without the TTL fields, which fingerprint the attacker hosts). The Isolation Forest reaches AUC $0.987\pm0.001$ (0.983--0.990 per type), because normal Modbus polling is very regular and the attacks (floods, scans) are far from it. Held-out detection is different. With DoS removed from training the classifier flags only 0.7\% of DoS test flows at 0.5 (AUC against normal 0.71), and for command injection and reconnaissance 8.1\% and 13.0\% although their AUC exceeds 0.999: the ranking is right but the scores sit far below any useful gate. Backdoor is found at 66.7\%. The command-injection and backdoor test sets hold only 74 and 63 flows, so those figures are imprecise.

\emph{Policy.} Table~\ref{tab:wustl21} gives full-policy results as means over the four attack types. With seen types the full policy admits 3.6--4.2\% of attack requests at T1--T3 (hiding 10.8--12.1\% for backdoor and 3.8--4.5\% for command injection, the two rarest types) and denies no legitimate request. With types withheld, acceptance rises to 75.4--77.8\% on average and 97.9--99.2\% for DoS at the three higher tiers; the threshold-only policy is not much worse (last column), so on unseen types the supervised score adds almost nothing, and an adaptive attacker sending its best 10\% or 1\% of flows is always accepted. The unsupervised score behaves very differently: the full policy admitted no attack request of any type at T1--T3, even the best 1\% of an adaptive attacker's flows, at the price of denying 1.5\%, 12.1\% and 22.3\% of legitimate requests at T1, T2 and T3. The two datasets with attack types agree on the main point and disagree on the detector: a supervised detector does not protect against unseen attack types, and one trained on normal traffic can, but only when normal traffic is as regular as in this testbed.

\emph{Limits of this result.} The favourable unsupervised numbers come from a laboratory testbed with machine-generated, nearly constant normal traffic and volumetric attacks from a handful of hosts, which makes anomaly detection easier than in a plant with operators, engineering workstations and maintenance windows. The rare attack types have very few flows, posture and network context are synthetic, and the pairing of a flow with a device is arbitrary.

\subsection{Baseline Comparison: Revocation Exposure}\label{sec:baselines}
On a shared logical clock we compare the per-request design with a session token (HMAC bearer token, TTL 60 or 300\,s) and PKI with a CRL refreshed every 60 or 300\,s, for 60 devices at 1 request/s with revocation times drawn from $U(100,500)$\,s. Table~\ref{tab:base} shows that the audited design accepts no request after revocation, as Proposition~\ref{prop:revoke} predicts, whereas the baselines accept on average 33 and 147 requests for the 60\,s and 300\,s windows (worst cases 59--299). The mechanism implies this result; the experiment quantifies it. Token validation is the cheapest per request (an HMAC, no public-key operation) and our design costs more per accepted request, which we do not hide. The fast path trades the zero for a bounded exposure of at most $\rho\Delta$ requests (Proposition~\ref{prop:stale}): for example, with $\Delta=5$\,s and one request per second the bound is 5 requests, still far below the 33--147 of token and CRL schemes.

Three further baselines answer the objection that tokens and CRLs are weak comparators (Table~\ref{tab:base2}; 30 runs on the same revocation times, 60 devices, 1 request/s). Short-lived certificates with lifetime $L$ and OCSP stapling with validity $V$ both bound exposure by the validity window, because a revoked device cannot renew. In our logical-clock model they have identical exposure (29.0 requests for a 60\,s window, 140.8 for 300\,s) and differ only in who must be online, so we report them as one mechanism. A centralised online policy decision point that checks a revocation database on every request matches the audited path with zero exposure, so exposure alone does not favour the blockchain design against it. The differences lie elsewhere. The central PDP is a single operator that alone can revoke, reinstate or silently alter its log; if it is unreachable for 60\,s it must either deny every request (86.7\% of legitimate requests served) or accept requests from devices revoked meanwhile (21.0 on average, up to 60); and its log has no external anchor. Our fast path faces the same outage trade-off for revocations that happen while the replica is blind (Section~\ref{sec:outage_eval}), so the claim we can support concerns governance and auditability, not availability. The simulation models validity windows only; real deployments add renewal failures, clock skew and responder caching.

\subsection{Architecture-Class Baselines and Sensitivity to the Trust Weights}\label{sec:prior_eval}
The baselines above model credential validity only. To compare against the \emph{design choices} of earlier blockchain schemes, we added two class models, built from how each family is described in the literature and run in the same simulator; they are not re-implementations of any named system. The first is a ledger registry that is checked at login, after which a session is trusted (the authenticate-once pattern \cite{wang2026medicaliot,james2024authz}). With sessions of 60\,s and 300\,s, a revoked device is still accepted for a mean of 29.0 and 129.2 requests (maxima 60 and 300), the same exposure as short-lived certificates: putting the registry on a ledger does not close the window unless every request is checked. The second is an on-chain policy with one static threshold of 0.55 for every resource, with no tiers, gate or strict mode. Table~\ref{tab:prior} shows that at the two critical tiers it accepts every partial-mimicry, stealthy and fully spoofed request, since a fully spoofing attacker scores at least 0.70 (Section~\ref{sec:security}), whereas the full policy accepts 0\%, 60.9\% (T2) and 13.6\% (T3) of the three attacks at a cost of 2.2\% and 5.2\% of legitimate requests. Compared with the earlier threshold-only rows, this isolates the value of the tiers, gate and strict mode over a single threshold.

The synthetic weights $(0.30,0.30,0.20,0.20)$ were chosen by hand, so we tested how much the results depend on them. We moved each weight by $\pm0.10$ (rescaling the others) and drew 200 random perturbations around the defaults. At T2 the legitimate denial rate stayed between 2.2\% and 2.3\%, and stealthy acceptance between 60.5\% and 60.9\%. At T3 legitimate denial ranged from 3.4\% to 11.8\% (median 5.7\%) and stealthy acceptance from 11.8\% to 14.8\%. Partial-mimicry and full-spoof acceptance stayed at 0\% in every variant at both tiers. The gate therefore holds for any reasonable weighting, while the false-denial cost at the highest tier is the quantity that depends on the weights and should be tuned per plant.

\subsection{Attack Detection}\label{sec:attacks}
Replay, forged-signature, use-after-revocation and unregistered-DID probes were each run in 500 trials and denied every time (one-sided 95\% upper bound on failure 0.6\%, rule of three). This validates the state-machine logic against these attacks; it is not evidence of unconditional security.

\subsection{Decentralized Revocation Governance}\label{sec:governance_eval}
Table~\ref{tab:governance} evaluates \texttt{RevocationGovernance} for $N\in\{5,7,9,11,15,21\}$ with 200 trials per size. Mean quorum latency on the testbed grows roughly linearly, from 0.56\,ms ($N=5$) to 1.91\,ms ($N=21$) for a cooperative group and from 0.93 to 3.67\,ms under maximal tolerated dissent ($N-q$ DENY voters), with 95\% CI half-widths of at most 0.10\,ms. Across all 1{,}200 trials the dissenting minority never prevented approval, and in 1{,}200 forced-revocation attempts with only $q-1$ approvals no revocation executed, consistent with Proposition~\ref{prop:gov}. On the EVM a proposal costs 72k gas, each vote 78k, and the final vote that triggers revocation 102k, so a quorum revocation costs 313k ($N=5$) to 800k gas ($N=21$): the governance cost grows with $q$, not with the fleet. Kumar et al.\ report 3--4\,s for registration and under 5\,s for revocation on a live PoA chain \cite{kumar2026privacy}; our figures are two to three orders of magnitude lower but measure compute only and are not comparable like for like. In a deployment each vote is a transaction, so revocation takes about $q$ block confirmations when votes are sequential, or one to two blocks if voters submit in parallel.

\subsection{End-to-End Evaluation on a Four-Validator QBFT Network}\label{sec:besu_eval}
To move beyond in-process execution we deployed the three contracts on a private Hyperledger Besu 25.7.0 network of four QBFT validators (2\,s block period, 4\,s round-change timeout, chain ID 2026, zero base fee) and drove it through JSON-RPC with signed transactions (\texttt{besu\_benchmark.js}, \texttt{besu\_fault.js}). In this first deployment the validators and load generator shared one two-vCPU container: blocks are proposed, voted on and committed by separate processes, but there is no wide-area delay and the processes compete for two cores, so the numbers describe consensus-bound behaviour on a small host. Section~\ref{sec:gcp_eval} repeats them across regions.

\textit{Latency and finality.} Table~\ref{tab:besu} shows that every state-changing call needs about one block period from submission to receipt (1.98--2.00\,s on average, p95 about 2.2\,s); QBFT finalizes in the block that includes the transaction, so a receipt is a final result. Revocation became visible on a second validator after 1.87\,s on average, and 30 of 30 audited \texttt{evaluateAccess} calls issued after that returned DENY. The replica was up to date before the receipt poll returned ($-117\pm75$\,ms, maximum +27\,ms), within polling jitter, so we read $\Delta\approx 0$ relative to the block period on a single host and, for the exposure bound $\rho\Delta$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:stale}, take the block period as the dominant term.

\textit{Throughput.} Table~\ref{tab:besu2} reports audited decisions sent as pre-signed transactions from ten sender accounts. All transactions were mined at the three offered loads, but sustained throughput was 11--18 tx/s and latency grew with the queue (mean 31.6\,s at 600 offered), 12--19 times below the gas-based ceiling of about 213 decisions per second (426 per 30M-gas block at 2\,s), so that ceiling is an upper bound, not an estimate. We attribute the gap to CPU saturation (four JVMs and the load generator on two cores) but did not profile it. The conclusion for the design is strengthened: routine requests must not be sent through consensus.

\textit{Governance and recall.} With five voters and quorum three, a revocation completed in 8.07\,s when votes were submitted one after another (each waiting for its block, so about four blocks) and in 4.03\,s when the votes were submitted together (about two blocks); all 20 proposals ended in revocation. A batch of 50 devices was revoked in one transaction (1.77M gas) and confirmed after 2.95\,s, and a batch of 200 devices (7.00M gas) after 6.13\,s; sampled revocations were visible on another validator. By comparison, registering 200 devices one transaction at a time took 399\,s. The batch and parallel-vote results confirm that on a block-timed chain, the number of blocks rather than the number of transactions determines recall and governance latency.

\textit{Fault tolerance.} With four validators QBFT tolerates one fault. With one validator stopped, registration latency had a mean of 2.83\,s (sd 2.00\,s, p95 6.16\,s, $n=8$) against 1.97\,s at baseline; the median was unchanged (1.91\,s), but rounds in which the stopped validator was the proposer had to time out. With two validators stopped no block was produced and a pending transaction was not mined within 20\,s; after both restarted it was mined after 78\,s. These single-transaction, small-sample runs show the qualitative behaviour (safe halt below the quorum, resumption after recovery), not a tight latency estimate. During a halt the fast path keeps deciding on its last replicated state, and the outage policy of Section~\ref{sec:outage} governs what is allowed.

\subsection{Four Validators in Four Regions}\label{sec:gcp_eval}
To remove the shared-CPU limitation and add real network distance, we repeated the benchmark on Google Cloud with one validator per e2-standard-2 virtual machine (2 vCPU, 8\,GB) in europe-west3, us-east4, asia-southeast1 and southamerica-east1 (same Besu 25.7.0 and QBFT parameters; Ubuntu 24.04, OpenJDK 21). The load generator ran in europe-west3 and submitted through the European validator; the revocation-visibility test queried the Asian and South American validators. We injected no artificial delay and did not record round-trip times, so the delay is whatever the provider's network gave on the day of the run (2026-10-12). Table~\ref{tab:gcp} sets the results next to the single-host run; both are single runs of the same script, with the sample sizes of Table~\ref{tab:besu}.

\textit{Finality and latency.} Block production stayed at 2.00\,s with no deviation, and the latency of \texttt{register}, \texttt{evaluateAccess} and \texttt{anchorDecisions} was unchanged within noise (1.98--2.00\,s, p95 2.16--2.18\,s): inter-region distance did not push consensus beyond one block period, so the 2\,s timer, not the network, set finality. The revocation receipt took 1.55\,s on average, less than on one host; the submissions are not aligned to the block timer and we do not read this as a speed-up.

\textit{Replica lag.} A revocation became visible on a validator in another region 1.76\,s after submission, and 30 of 30 audited decisions on a second remote validator issued afterwards returned DENY. Unlike on one host, the replica now trailed the receipt: by $+212\pm145$\,ms on average (p95 362\,ms, maximum 495\,ms, minimum $-39$\,ms). Taking the largest value as an estimate for this network, the time between a revocation request and its visibility to a remote replica was about one block period plus 0.2--0.5\,s, so the exposure bound $\rho\Delta$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:stale} for a fast-path gateway is dominated by the block period but no longer negligible against the network. This is one run on one provider's backbone; a plant link over a public network or a satellite path would give a larger $\Delta$ and has to be measured where it is deployed.

\textit{Throughput.} All 100, 300 and 600 pre-signed audited decisions were mined, at 30.8, 34.4 and 50.1 tx/s against 17.8, 16.0 and 11.3 on one host; the mean latency at 600 offered fell from 31.6\,s to 8.6\,s (p95 10.7\,s). Throughput still rose with offered load, so we did not find the saturation point: 50 tx/s is a lower bound for this deployment, still 4--7 times below the gas-based ceiling of 213 decisions per second. The conclusion is unchanged: routine requests must not wait for consensus.

\subsection{Static Analysis and Randomised Invariant Tests}\label{sec:assure}
Slither (102 detectors) reported 11 findings on the three contracts: one medium (a strict equality on \texttt{registeredAt==0}, the intended existence test), four low (timestamp comparisons, a missing zero-address check on the oracle in the constructor, and an event after an external call to our own registry), one informational finding per signature-recovery routine and four optimisation hints. No reentrancy, access-control or arithmetic finding was reported. Reading the code for what Slither does not check, we found one genuine gap: with a zero oracle address or a zero registered signer, an invalid signature (for which \texttt{ecrecover} returns the zero address) would pass. We added a zero-oracle check in the constructor and rejected a zero recovered address at registration (+62 gas for \texttt{registerDevice}). Signature malleability is harmless because replay protection is keyed on (device, nonce).

Three new tests complement the 13 unit tests: the zero-oracle and zero-signer cases, and a randomised state-machine test (300 steps, three seeds, six devices) of suspend, reinstate, revoke and access operations against an independent model. It checks that revocation is terminal, that reinstatement succeeds only from suspension, that an inactive device or reused (device, nonce) pair is never allowed, and that on-chain status matches the model. All 16 tests pass. This is bounded random testing, not a proof or audit; the contracts are not formally verified.

\subsection{Degraded Operation During an Outage}\label{sec:outage_eval}

The scenario is deterministic given its parameters, so it illustrates the trade-off rather than measuring it: the operator chooses explicitly between unsafe acceptances and lost availability.
